Reading Response 1: Shoshana Zuboff, The Age of Surveillance Capitalism (Chapter 1)

In Chapter One, Zuboff argues that surveillance capitalism is a new economic system where companies treat human experience as raw material that can be turned into data. What stood out to me is that she’s not just saying companies collect data; she's saying they extract something extra from our behavior, which she calls “behavioral surplus,” and then use it to predict and influence future actions. That makes this bigger than just a privacy issue. It becomes about power.

I think this reading pushes us to think beyond the idea that platforms are just tools. Zuboff makes it clear that surveillance capitalism is not just about technology; it’s about economic choices. These companies built systems where data extraction is the core business model. That changes how we understand platforms like Google or Facebook. They aren’t neutral spaces; they’re environments designed to observe, categorize, and shape behavior.

What I found interesting is her claim that this system works by modifying behavior at scale. If platforms are constantly predicting what we’ll click or buy next, then they are also subtly guiding those outcomes. It makes me question how much of what we think is “our choice” is actually influenced by algorithmic design.

At the same time, I wonder if Zuboff sometimes presents the system as too overwhelming. People still use these platforms strategically. We’re not completely passive. So I’m curious where the line is between influence and control.

Overall, I think this chapter feels less like a complaint about Big Tech and more like a warning about how economic systems shape digital life. It pushes us to ask who has control over knowledge and behavior in a data driven society.

Reading Response 2: Jon Keegan, “Facebook Got Rid of Racial Ad Categories. Or Did It?”

This article looks at Facebook’s decision to remove racial ad categories and questions whether that actually solved anything. Even though Facebook eliminated direct categories like “African American multicultural affinity" The Markup found that advertisers still use cultural interests like “African-American culture,” “BET,” and “Black History Month” to target specific racial groups. The chart in the article shows how these cultural labels act as racial proxies.

What I think is important here is that the issue isn’t just about one bad category. It’s about how the whole advertising system works. If advertisers can always find workarounds, then removing a few labels doesn’t really change the structure. It just changes the language. That makes this feel less like a policy fix and more like surface level reform.

This reading highlights how algorithms and data categories shape social outcomes. If ads related to housing, jobs, or credit can be directed toward certain racial groups (even indirectly), then digital platforms are influencing real-world inequality. What also stood out to me is that Facebook’s Ad Library doesn’t show detailed targeting information. That means researchers and the public can’t fully see how ads are being distributed. So transparency is limited.

One question this raises for me is whether targeted advertising can ever be fully separated from discrimination. If the system is built on sorting people into behavioral groups, it seems like those groupings will always overlap with race, class, or other social identities.

This article makes it clear that algorithmic systems don’t have to explicitly mention race to reproduce racial patterns. And that’s what makes the issue more complicated than just deleting a category.
